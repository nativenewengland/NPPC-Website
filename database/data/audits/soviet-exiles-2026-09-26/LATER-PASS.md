# Later Soviet-exile cases — second pass, September 26, 2026

This pass concentrated on Americans who entered the Soviet Union from 1960 through its dissolution in 1991. It screened military desertion, intelligence investigations, antiwar defection, formal Soviet asylum, and confinement after defection. The live database was checked without the normal public scope so hidden records would also be found.

## Publication status

Published September 26, 2026. Seventeen profiles were added in this continuation: the seven later-period cases below, nine atomic-era and embassy cases, and Alexander Kazem-Bek. The live database verification found all 17 public, marked for historical exile, and linked to at least one case with an exile start. Every public URL returned HTTP 200.

## Later-period profiles published

- **Bruce Frederick Davis** — U.S. Army soldier who left his West German post in August 1960, spent a month in East Germany, and entered the USSR. In Soviet press he attributed his defection to disillusionment with U.S. foreign and military policy. He returned to U.S. military control in July 1963.
- **Joseph Dutkanicz** — U.S. Army sergeant and admitted KGB recruit who left West Germany for the USSR in June 1960 as a Western security investigation approached him. He asked to return in 1962 but died in Lviv in November 1963 before repatriation.
- **Vladimir Sloboda** — naturalized U.S. citizen assigned to the Army's 513th Military Intelligence Group. He crossed into East Germany and requested Soviet asylum in August 1960 amid Army counterintelligence activity. Soviet media presented his statements against U.S. military and intelligence policy.
- **Victor Norris Hamilton** — naturalized American and former NSA Arabic cryptanalyst who went to the USSR in 1962. His disclosures appeared in Soviet media in 1963. Soviet authorities confined him in psychiatric institutions, including Special Hospital No. 5 from 1971; he was rediscovered there in 1992 after more than twenty years.
- **Harold M. Koch** — former Chicago Catholic priest who sought Soviet asylum in 1966 and publicly said the move protested the Vietnam War. He returned to the United States about three months later in December 1966.
- **Theodore and Cheryl Branch** — Pennsylvania couple who entered the USSR as tourists in November 1987, requested asylum as a rejection of capitalism, and received it in January 1988. They worked on English-language broadcasting, returned to the United States in September 1988, and unsuccessfully sought to go back to the USSR.

## Additional profiles published

- **Oscar Seborer, Stuart Seborer and Miriam Zeitlin Seborer** — the Seborer group secretly entered the Soviet bloc in 1952 and moved to Moscow in 1957. Miriam’s record separately documents her attempt to return, Soviet arrest and threats, internal exile to Alma-Ata, and eventual departure on December 29, 1969.
- **George Koval** — left the United States in October 1948 as counterintelligence scrutiny approached and remained in Moscow. Espionage conclusions are attributed to the later archival record.
- **Anatoly P. Kotloby** — fled after FBI questioning in 1964 and was later entrapped in a Soviet currency case and sentenced to eight years after the KGB suspected, but could not prove, that he was a double agent.
- **James McMillin** — refused repatriation from his Moscow embassy cryptographic assignment on May 15, 1948, publicly invoking opposition to American capitalist policy while embassy officials emphasized his relationship with Galina Dunaeva Biconish.
- **John Discoe Smith** — resigned from the State Department, disappeared by mid-1960, and surfaced in the USSR in 1967. His later CIA allegations and the U.S. denials are both attributed.
- **Annabelle Bucar** — resigned from the Moscow embassy in February 1948, cited both her favorable view of Soviet life and her relationship with Konstantin Lapshin, and remained in Moscow.
- **Orest Stephen Makar** — renounced recently acquired U.S. citizenship and returned to the USSR in December 1956; the profile treats espionage as an allegation because no completed prosecution was located.
- **Alexander Kazem-Bek** — a Russian émigré educator who applied to return in 1954 and left the United States at the end of 1956. His profile records his published political explanation while making clear that he was not an American-born exile.

## Screened controls

The HSCA list also contains Robert Webster, Morris and Mollie Block, Harold Citrynell, Shirley Dubinsky, Martin Greendlinger, Nicholas Petrulli, Libero Ricciardelli and other people who went voluntarily for employment, family, citizenship, or personal reasons without a political or coercive trigger comparable to the selected cases. Lee Harvey Oswald was also retained as a control rather than a political-exile addition. The asylum-practice study reports no additional known formal Soviet grants of asylum to Americans after the Branch case and before the USSR's dissolution.

## Leads still lacking corroboration

- **William Donal Adkins** appears in a derivative defector table as a U.S. Army officer who went to the USSR in 1954, citing only a short Associated Press item titled “Yank Officer Picks Soviet.” No independent account establishing his identity, act, motive, or outcome was located.
- **William Turner** appears in the same derivative table for 1954, but the available citation trail does not establish which William Turner it means. Search results also merge unrelated people with that common name.
- **J. W. Wright** appears only as a name and year in later lists. No independent contemporary or archival source was located.

These three were not added. A list entry alone is insufficient to infer military service, political motivation, Soviet asylum, continuing exile, or an eventual outcome.
