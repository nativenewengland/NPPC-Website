# Washington Area Spark Flickr screening

The public Flickr API returned 5,355 unique public photo records for the Washington Area Spark account on 2026-09-27. The account header displayed 5,356 photos. All 5,355 API-visible records were downloaded and screened through their titles, descriptions, tags, dates, and license metadata; the one-item difference could not be retrieved through the public API.

The first publication batch covers the Puerto Rican Nationalist Party sedition-trial album. It adds sixteen convicted defendants missing from the live database and joins Santiago González Castro's 1955 federal conviction to his existing profile. Evidence was cross-checked against *United States v. Lebron*, 222 F.2d 531 (2d Cir. 1955), the Department of Justice's June 1954 United States Attorneys Bulletin, the Washington Area Spark album captions, and later Puerto Rican historical records.

After the rights holders authorized reuse, a separate photo pass matched the 89 new profiles against the account's original photographs. Forty profiles received individually traceable photographs. The crops use only caption-identified subjects; archival borders and excess scene area were removed without generative editing. Profiles were left unchanged when the Flickr post merely mentioned a person, showed an unlabelled crowd, reproduced a flyer, or did not locate the named person within a group. Each updated profile links to its exact Flickr post, identifies Washington Area Spark as the archive, and notes that the documentary photograph is used with permission. The Gellert caption describes its identifications as tentative, and the two corresponding profile credits preserve that qualification.

The direct-action batch adds twenty-four people whose arrest or confinement is explicitly described in the captions and corroborating records. It covers the 1960 Annapolis and Arlington sit-ins, the 1934 Capitol restaurant campaign, anti-freeway and welfare-rights actions, gay-rights and antiwar civil disobedience, and early anti-fascist/anti-imperialist pickets. Where the available record establishes an arrest but not a conviction or sentence, the case says that directly and carries no invented release date or duration.

The follow-up batch adds fourteen more named defendants from anti-apartheid, anti-Klan, anti-fascist, free-press, and socialist organizing. Independent reporting corrected one repeated caption claim: Eleanor Holmes Norton spoke to reporters outside the South African Embassy on November 21, 1984 but was not arrested, so no prisoner profile was created from that claim. Likewise, Desmond Tutu was not arrested at the Washington embassy rally described in the account; his separate 1988 arrest in South Africa was outside this U.S.-custody screening batch.

Four later batches add thirty-five more missing people. They cover the 1978 Virginia ERA protest; Rose Smith's sixteen-month pretrial confinement in the New Haven Panther prosecution; all seven named adult appellants from the March 11, 1965 White House Selma sit-in; Gandhi and Indian-independence picketers; Leonard Matlovich's 1987 AIDS-policy protest; maritime organizer Patrick Whalen; the 1972 University of Maryland antiwar prosecutions; the Three Sisters Bridge fight; and eleven independently verified Free South Africa Movement arrests. The White House appellate opinion supplied four names that were not present in the Flickr caption, illustrating why the review followed named events into outside records instead of limiting the search to caption wording.

In total, the publication scripts add **89 profiles and 91 cases**, including one new case and corrected name for the existing Santiago González Castro profile. Every script refuses to run if a same-name profile already exists, creates a SQLite backup before changing the live database, and verifies the resulting profile and case counts.

Important exclusions include Muhammad Ali, whose five-year draft sentence was stayed and ultimately reversed before he entered prison; Roger Priest, whose court-martial imposed a bad-conduct discharge and reduction in rank but no confinement; and Philip Hirschkop, whose contempt sentence was stayed on appeal and overturned. The review also rejected ordinary-crime references, people imprisoned solely by foreign governments outside a U.S.-custody case, people mentioned only as supporters of prisoners, and caption names that could not be tied to the described arrest.

Several group captions remain anonymous even after follow-up searching. Examples include five of the forty seamen who served thirty days after the 1936 German Embassy protest, five Local 471 cafeteria strikers sentenced in 1948, six Gay Activists Alliance protesters arrested at the Iwo Jima Memorial in 1972, and many participants in mass Mayday and anti-apartheid arrests. Those events are documented in the screening material, but no individual profile was created without a recoverable name. A separate Robert Ferraro mentioned in the 1972 University of Maryland material was not merged into the existing Irish-republican gun-running profile because the shared name does not establish that they are the same person.
