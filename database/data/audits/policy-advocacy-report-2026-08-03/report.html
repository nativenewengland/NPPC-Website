Policy report — August 3, 2026

Political-prisoner advocacy in the United States rests on years of patient, mostly unpaid work. Organizations maintain prisoner lists, publish news from inside, organize letter-writing, raise commissary money, support families, prepare parole letters and respond when someone is denied medical care. Without that work, many political prisoners would be far more isolated than they already are.

But persistence is not the same as preparedness. Too much of the movement still operates case by case and emergency by emergency. A transfer is discovered after mail is returned. A medical crisis becomes public after treatment has already been delayed. A parole campaign begins when the hearing is close. A newly charged activist may spend months without a clear point of contact. A person approaches release without housing, identification, health care or a reliable support team.

These are not failures of commitment. They are failures of structure. The movement has created many strong projects, but it has not yet created a dependable, shared system that finds cases early, assigns responsibility, tracks deadlines, verifies urgent information and stays with people through release.

Scope and standard

This report reviews the public-facing work and published procedures of organizations supporting U.S.-held political prisoners and prisoners of war. It also reviews federal prison, compassionate-release and clemency procedures that advocates must navigate. It is a systems audit, not an accusation that any organization or volunteer is acting in bad faith.

Public information has limits. The absence of a protocol from an organization’s website does not prove that no internal protocol exists. Different groups also use different definitions of “political prisoner,” often for principled reasons. Our finding is narrower: as of this report’s date, the public record does not show a movement-wide intake, triage and case-management system with shared minimum standards and transparent measures of performance.

What the movement already does

There is a substantial base to build on. NYC Anarchist Black Cross has held regular political-prisoner letter-writing events and circulated updates for years. Its public archive says that hard copies of updates were mailed to imprisoned comrades beginning in 2011, expanded to all U.S.-held political prisoners and prisoners of war in 2013, and later became distributed among several support crews and individuals.

The Anarchist Black Cross Federation’s Warchest Program provides recurring financial support to vetted prisoners and has also paid emergency legal and release expenses. Its December 2025 report listed 17 monthly recipients receiving $50 each, documented a $2,500 emergency legal disbursement and acknowledged that the number of supporters and the resources available remained small.

The Jericho Movement maintains public case information and issues calls for parole, commutation and other forms of support. Individual defense committees have built sustained campaigns around particular people. These efforts matter. The problem is that they do not add up to a common operating system. Information, money, legal skill and public attention remain unevenly distributed, and too much depends on whether a family member or volunteer already knows whom to call.

Finding 1: There is no common front door

A person who believes a prosecution or imprisonment is political has no widely recognized intake route. Referrals arrive through personal relationships, social media, attorneys, family members or local organizers. Some receive immediate attention; others remain unknown outside their community. There is no shared acknowledgment deadline, standard evidence request or transparent review process.

This creates two risks. The first is neglect: a credible case may be missed because it does not reach the right network. The second is inconsistency: public recognition may depend more on proximity and familiarity than on a stated standard. A national movement should be able to receive a referral, preserve the evidence, identify urgent needs and explain what it can or cannot do.

Finding 2: Advocacy begins too late

Many support campaigns become visible after conviction, a severe sentence, a medical emergency or years of incarceration. By then, important records may be scattered, witnesses harder to reach and public understanding shaped almost entirely by the government’s account.

Proactive advocacy begins earlier. It monitors politically charged arrests and prosecutions, contacts defense teams without interfering with legal strategy, preserves public records, prepares plain-language case summaries and maps the deadlines that will govern appeals, prison placement, parole, clemency and release. It does not wait for a crisis to decide whether a case deserves attention.

Finding 3: The movement lacks a shared operational picture

Existing directories are valuable, but they often answer different questions and are updated on different schedules. A name may appear on one list and not another. Release status, facility, medical condition, legal posture and contact rules can change quickly. When groups disagree about a case, there is rarely a visible process for comparing evidence, recording uncertainty or correcting a public entry.

A directory is not a case-management system. Advocates need a shared record of who verified each fact, when it was checked, what deadline comes next, who is responsible for follow-up and what information may safely be made public. Without that structure, every new emergency requires volunteers to reconstruct the case from the beginning.

Finding 4: Medical advocacy is driven by crisis

Federal oversight reports show why waiting is dangerous. In its inspection of Federal Correctional Institution Sheridan, the Justice Department’s Office of Inspector General found hundreds of pending laboratory and imaging orders, canceled outside appointments and a dental waitlist on which many people had waited two years or more. The details concern one institution, but the lesson is broader: delay accumulates quietly before it becomes a public emergency.

Advocacy groups often mobilize effectively once a medical crisis is known. What is missing is a common prevention protocol: regular health check-ins where possible, consent-based records, a method for verifying reports, escalation thresholds, assigned medical and legal contacts, and a plan for communicating with family and the public. A person should not have to become critically ill before the movement builds the file needed to advocate for care.

Finding 5: Material support is too uneven

Commissary funds, postage, phone calls, books, travel, legal documents and family visits are basic advocacy infrastructure. Yet support often depends on the size and reach of a person’s existing campaign. Recurring programs such as the Warchest demonstrate what dependable support can look like, while their limited scale shows the gap between a durable model and the need it is asked to meet.

The movement has no common minimum support standard and no shared emergency reserve. As a result, the best-known cases can raise substantial help while less visible prisoners and their families bear routine costs alone. Proactive advocacy requires predictable material support, not only fundraising after an emergency appeal.

Finding 6: Legal, parole and clemency work remains siloed

Parole, compassionate release and executive clemency are document-heavy processes. Federal compassionate-release requests require the grounds for release and a proposed release plan. Clemency applications require supporting records, proceed through investigation and review, and carry no guarantee of a decision within a particular administration. These are not campaigns that can be assembled responsibly in the final weeks.

Public appeals are most useful when they support a case file already being built. Advocacy organizations should identify eligibility dates early, gather records with the prisoner’s consent, locate qualified counsel, prepare family and community support, and create a release plan before a hearing or filing becomes urgent. At present, that preparation varies widely from case to case.

Finding 7: Families and release planning are treated as secondary

Families frequently become researchers, fundraisers, public spokespeople and crisis coordinators without training or relief. They also absorb the costs of calls, travel, documents and care. A proactive system would ask what the family wants, designate a reliable contact, protect private information and avoid making relatives repeat the same history to multiple organizations.

Release is also an advocacy phase. Identification, housing, income, medical appointments, digital access, transportation and media boundaries should be planned in advance. A campaign that ends at the prison gate leaves the released person to manage the consequences of confinement alone.

Finding 8: Public accountability is too weak

Political-prisoner groups often publish urgent calls and individual victories, but rarely publish service measures: how many referrals were received, how quickly they were answered, how many cases have an assigned contact, how many records were corrected, or how many upcoming hearings have a support plan. Resource constraints are real, but they make prioritization and honest reporting more necessary.

Transparent measures would not reduce people to numbers. They would show where requests are going unanswered and give supporters something concrete to build. They would also allow organizations to acknowledge limits rather than creating an expectation of help they cannot reliably provide.

A minimum proactive-advocacy standard

NPPC recommends that political-prisoner organizations adopt the following shared baseline:

  1. A national intake desk. Provide one public referral route, acknowledge submissions within 72 hours and conduct an initial evidence and urgency review within 14 days.
  2. A sourced, reviewable registry. Record the basis for inclusion, source dates, current status and unresolved conflicts. Give prisoners or their designated representatives a way to submit corrections and appeal a decision.
  3. A named case lead and backup. Every active case should have a responsible contact, a backup contact and a recorded last-contact date. Assignment does not require one group to perform every service; it requires someone to make sure the case does not disappear between organizations.
  4. An early-warning calendar. Track trials, filing deadlines, appeals, parole eligibility, hearings, transfers, expected release dates and recurring medical needs. Alerts should reach the people responsible before action becomes urgent.
  5. A medical escalation protocol. With the prisoner’s consent, document the request for care, verify what can be verified, preserve records and set clear thresholds for administrative, legal and public escalation.
  6. A material-support floor. Establish a dependable minimum for communication and basic needs, plus a shared emergency fund with written eligibility and reporting rules.
  7. A legal, parole and clemency pipeline. Begin preparation at least 12 months before a known eligibility or release date whenever possible. Maintain a referral network of lawyers, investigators, record researchers and reentry providers.
  8. Family and reentry plans. Ask each prisoner whom the movement may contact, what information may be shared and what support is needed. Create a release plan covering identification, housing, health care, income, transportation and public communication.
  9. Quarterly public reporting. Publish response times, open referrals, assigned cases, upcoming deadlines, urgent gaps and corrections. Protect private information while reporting whether the system is functioning.

Twelve-month implementation goals

A coalition adopting this standard should be able to meet five visible goals within one year:

  • Acknowledge at least 90 percent of new referrals within 72 hours and complete an initial review within 14 days.
  • Assign a lead and backup to every accepted active case, with a current last-contact date and next-action field.
  • Issue a verified urgent medical alert within 24 hours when an agreed escalation threshold is met.
  • Begin parole, clemency and release planning at least 12 months before a known date whenever the timeline permits.
  • Publish a quarterly report showing performance, unmet needs and corrections to the public record.

Conclusion

The political-prisoner movement does not lack dedication. It lacks enough shared infrastructure to turn dedication into reliable early action. Volunteers have kept correspondence moving, sent money, maintained public memory and rallied around crises. The next obligation is to connect that work through standards that do not depend on personal access or last-minute attention.

Proactive advocacy means finding a case before it is forgotten, preparing before a deadline, checking on health before a crisis, and planning for freedom before the prison door opens. Political prisoners and their families should be able to know who is responsible, what comes next and when they will hear back. Anything less leaves too much to chance.
